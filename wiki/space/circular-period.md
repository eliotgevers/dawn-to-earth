## The two-body classroom model

For an ideal circular orbit, the time for one revolution is

$$
T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{\mu}}.
$$

Here $T$ is period in seconds, $r$ is the distance from the central body’s center in metres, and $\mu$ is its gravitational parameter in cubic metres per second squared. The factor $2\pi$ is dimensionless. The ratio under the square root has units of seconds squared. Altitude is measured above a reference surface; it is different from the radius in the equation.

This relationship assumes one dominant central body and an unchanging circular radius. It describes a classroom orbit and omits perturbations. A period alone is also insufficient to predict when a particular ground location will next be observable.

![A circular path around a central body showing the radius measured from the center to the orbiting body.](/wiki-diagrams/circular-period.svg)

*Original classroom diagram for Dawn to Earth. Distances are illustrative.*

For two circular orbits around the same idealized central body, the common gravitational parameter cancels:

$$
\frac{T_2}{T_1} = \left(\frac{r_2}{r_1}\right)^{3/2}.
$$

The periods $T_1$ and $T_2$ use the same time units, and the radii $r_1$ and $r_2$ use the same length units. Both ratios are dimensionless. For example, doubling the radius gives a period about 2.83 times as long.

## Fictional example

For two imaginary circular orbits around the same body, the outer circle has the longer period. No flight parameters are needed to compare them.
