## Orbit and orientation

Launch places a satellite in an initial orbit. Some civil satellites carry propulsion so they can later change that path, maintain it despite small disturbances, respond to a predicted close approach, or support end-of-mission disposal. The need depends on what the mission asks the spacecraft to do. Some missions can meet their objectives without onboard propulsion.

**Orbit** describes where the spacecraft travels. **Attitude** describes which way it faces. An instrument may need precise pointing even if the spacecraft has no ability to change its orbit. Orientation can be managed by other parts of the spacecraft. An educational mission description should make clear which of these capabilities it is discussing.

## Public civil spacecraft example

Dawn lists CubeDrive for CubeSat-class spacecraft and SatDrive as a modular system for larger satellites. Its November 2024 announcement about lunar data-relay satellites identifies Dawn as the propulsion-module supplier. Blue Canyon Technologies was responsible for the buses and for integrating the communications payloads. The relay mission illustrates how a subsystem supports a spacecraft’s purpose while the payload delivers the service. See [Dawn Aerospace’s civil satellite work](dawn-civil-satellite-work.md) for sources and the division of work.

## Fictional example

The fictional Rimu Surveyor mission lists keeping its observation orbit and pointing its instrument as separate capabilities.
